Capture runs only with the underwriter’s knowledge. She can see everything held about her on one screen, at any time, and pause it in one click. No approval, no reason given, no notification to anyone. This is written into the contract, not just the product.
the horizon· where this goes

A house model that carries cues the table cannot.

over time, on your book, a model of your own experts can begin to outprice the generic industry model — because it carries the cues your actuarial table never had a column for. we treat that as a result to prove on your data, not a claim to make on a homepage.

and the underwriter whose judgment the model keeps reusing can earn from contributing it — a recognition mechanism a carrier chooses to run, not a feature we administer. it flips capture from something seniors guard into something they want to feed, which is the difference between a capture product that adopts and one that dies on the vine.

what Tom does not do: invent risk appetite, replace the underwriter of record, or claim a loss-ratio number before it has earned one on your book. the model is only ever as good as the desk it learns from — which is precisely why it is yours, and not ours.

the stake· why an MGA feels this first

A carrier that loses an underwriter loses a good employee. An MGA can lose the book.

Your capacity provider is, in the end, underwriting your underwriters. The binder gets renewed because the loss ratio holds, and the loss ratio holds because a few people keep making good calls that nobody wrote down. When one of them leaves, you have a conversation to have, and the only evidence you can bring is a results history that may not repeat.

A model of how your desk underwrites is the first asset that survives the person. It belongs to the MGA. Not to the underwriter, and not to us.

fit· four questions

Is your desk a fit?

Four questions. If any one of them is a no, we’d rather find out now than three months in.

  1. Does your underwriter set the price, or work inside a matrix?

    We read the distance between the technical price and the price actually written. If your underwriters can only move a rate up, or only inside a fixed band, that distance gets clipped at the edges and we can’t read it cleanly. The method needs full rate-setting authority.

  2. How many submissions does each underwriter see in a week?

    Around five a week over two years is enough to work with. Much fewer and there isn’t enough to learn from. Several hundred a day and the work has usually turned into rules rather than judgement, and something simpler than us will do a better job.

  3. How far back do the submission documents survive?

    Not just the bound price. The application, the file notes, the reasoning. We need roughly two years of them.

  4. How long has this underwriter been writing this book, here?

    The one nobody expects. A brilliant underwriter who joined eighteen months ago left her decision history at her last employer. We can’t read a trail that isn’t there.
