the problem

Behind the numbers

Once it’s set up, an AI triage agent can close the alerts it judges harmless. Microsoft’s phishing triage agent, for example, resolves the emails it judges non-malicious, so analysts don’t spend time reviewing them.2

Only one in five security leaders leans on the vendor’s number.3 Re-checking everything is prudent, but it eats the time the AI was meant to save. And re-checking with the AI’s verdict on screen catches far fewer of its misses.

agreement

Why “it agrees with our analysts” isn’t enough

The proof most often offered for an AI analyst is how often it agrees with a human one. Here are two published figures.

99.7%

How often an AI agent agreed with human analysts in one vendor’s customer trials, across 138,475 alerts.4

67%

How often analysts agreed with an AI agent’s verdict in a 14-month study at a university SOC: 72 of 108 tickets. Of the seven outright clashes, only one was a clear AI error. The other six were judgment calls.5

Both can be true. Different AIs, different alerts and, above all, different people to agree with.

Analysts disagree with each other, and anyone can call the same alert differently on a different day. So “agreement with an analyst” is a ruler that moves.

We fix the ruler first. Where outcomes exist, we use them. Where they don’t, we use your team’s steady judgment.

That’s how your team decides on a good day. We call it the house view. Then we measure the AI against that.

reporting clocks

A wrong “benign” can miss a deadline

Regulated firms run on reporting clocks:

  • Six hours in India, under CERT-In7 and RBI8 rules.
  • Four hours in the EU under DORA once a major incident is classified, and no later than 24 hours after you become aware of it.9
  • One hour for firms regulated by MAS in Singapore.10

If the AI closes a real incident as harmless, nobody knows the clock should be running.

So for regulated teams we add one more number: reporting-clock exposure. For each alert type, it shows how often the AI clears alerts your team would have escalated as a possible reportable incident.

data handling

What we touch, and what we don’t

We do

  • Read decision records, read-only.
  • Run inside your cloud, in your region. Nothing leaves it except the reports you choose to share.
  • Scramble (hash) analyst and user IDs with a key you hold.
  • Report at team level only.
  • Recommend where the AI may act alone. You decide.

We never

  • Write to your systems, or change an alert, a rule or a setting.
  • Read email bodies, attachments, files or packet captures.
  • Score, rank or report on individual analysts. We judge agents, not analysts.

Leaving: Revoke our access and we stop reading.

regulation

What your regulator is starting to ask

Today, the hard deadlines are for reporting incidents. On AI, most rules are new, in draft, or start later. But they all point the same way: you stay accountable for the AI you use, including AI from a vendor.

India

  • Draft
  • In force
  • RBI’s draft guidance on model risk management (24 June 2026) covers third-party and AI models. It asks banks to validate them independently, “notwithstanding any validation, certification or assurance provided by the third-party provider”.11 It also asks for continuous monitoring of drift. It is still a draft.
  • RBI’s cyber directions, issued on 31 July 2026 with immediate effect, require banks to report cyber incidents within six hours of detection.8 CERT-In’s clock is also six hours.7

Where Tacit helps: outcome-based evidence your own validation team can use and challenge; drift monitoring; reporting-clock exposure.

Singapore

  • Final
  • Applies 2027
  • MAS’s Guidelines on AI Risk Management (final, 7 October 2026) keep firms responsible for risks from third-party AI.12 Governance expectations apply from 7 October 2027, and lifecycle controls from October 2028.
  • MAS’s SAFR paper (first published July 2026, updated 9 October 2026; voluntary) sets out four outcomes for an AI agent’s action: Auto-Execute, Observe, Escalate and Deny.6
  • Firms must notify MAS within one hour of discovering a relevant IT security incident.10

Where Tacit helps: measured evidence on third-party AI; an autonomy map that uses SAFR’s four outcomes; reporting-clock exposure.

European Union

  • In force
  • Supervisory
  • DORA has applied since 17 January 2025. It covers ICT third-party risk. The first notice of a major incident is due within four hours of classifying it, and no later than 24 hours after becoming aware of it.9
  • In July 2026 the ECB asked the banks it directly supervises for action plans on AI-enabled cyber threats, including AI-enabled defence, with plans due by 31 October 2026.13

Where Tacit helps: oversight records for the AI in your detection and response; monitoring of third-party AI.

Canada

  • Applies 1 May 2027
  • OSFI’s Guideline E-23 on model risk management takes effect on 1 May 2027. It covers AI models, and models from third-party vendors.14

Where Tacit helps: outcome analysis and ongoing monitoring of AI triage models.
